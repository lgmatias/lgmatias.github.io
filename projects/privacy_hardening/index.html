Privacy Hardening

A multi device build for a strong privacy posture, with minimal trust given to proprietary software

Most of my security work sits on the enterprise side, where the job is protecting an organization's data from the people outside it. Privacy hardening asks a different question. It asks how much a single person leaks to the parties already inside the tent, and how little proprietary software you have to trust in order to get through an ordinary day. That question is an important part of security and it does not get explored much in this industry.

This is an ongoing build across several devices. The organizing idea is that a threat model belongs to a device rather than to a person. No single machine is good at everything, so instead of pushing one laptop toward a threat model it cannot serve, I split the work across hardware.

Where it stands today. The laptop and the phone are mostly done. Account and credential hardening is the half I am working on now. The wireless detection hardware is not acquired yet, and the ghost laptop is still a design rather than a build. I would rather show the shape of the whole thing and be clear about which parts are finished than present a plan as a result.

Three categories of adversary

The build sorts adversaries into three categories, and each category gets the device that can actually answer it. The reason to write them down is that most privacy advice fails by mixing them, either burning usability on a threat you do not face or reaching for a tool that was never built for the threat you do.

Category Adversary Answered by
1 Mass surveillance and ad tech and data brokers. Everyone faces this, constantly. Privacy laptop and GrapheneOS phone
2 A targeted adversary without state resources. A thief. A stalker. A doxxer. The same two devices, plus the detection kit
3 A resourceful state level adversary. The ghost laptop, and nothing else

The privacy laptop

Status: mostly built.

The daily driver runs CachyOS, which is a performance distribution rather than a privacy one. That is worth saying plainly instead of quietly leaving it out. It ships aggressive compiler builds and extra repositories from a small team, which means more code churn and more parties to trust than upstream Arch. What makes the machine defensible is the layer I put on top of it rather than the base image underneath.

  • LUKS2 full disk encryption.
  • UEFI Secure Boot enrolled with my own keys.
  • AppArmor for mandatory access control.
  • nftables as the host firewall.
  • USBGuard, so an unattended port is not an open door.

This machine is a staging ground. The endgame is a ThinkPad T480 running free firmware, at which point the base stops being something I have to compensate for. Until then the hardening layer carries the weight, and I would rather run a compromised base I understand than a better one I have not finished migrating to.

Firmware stays stock here, on purpose

The daily driver keeps its UEFI Secure Boot and TPM instead of moving to free firmware. Measured boot and a TPM backed unlock produce real evidence that somebody tampered with the machine while it was out of my hands, and that is exactly what the category 2 adversary does. Stripping the firmware here would be symmetry rather than security, because I would give up measured boot to gain something this device does not need. Firmware freedom belongs on the appliance that actually depends on it.

Memory at rest

The LUKS master key sits in RAM the entire time the disk is unlocked. On suspend the RAM stays powered, so a stolen laptop that is merely locked still carries that key, recoverable through a cold boot or a DMA attack over Thunderbolt. Hibernate writes RAM out to swap and cuts power. Shutdown is stronger still. The catch is that the swap has to sit inside the encrypted volume, because otherwise hibernation writes out in the clear precisely what full disk encryption was there to protect.

The next step is enrolling a YubiKey as a FIDO2 unlock factor through systemd-cryptenroll, while keeping a passphrase keyslot as recovery so that a lost key is not a lockout. That factor is firmware agnostic, so it carries forward to the T480 later, unlike the TPM binding it sits alongside.

Three browser tiers that never cross streams

This is the part of the build doing the most work for the least money, and it is mostly discipline rather than software.

The identity tier is not anonymity and it was never meant to be. Accounts tied to my legal name cannot be made anonymous, and pretending they can only corrupts the work that genuinely is anonymous by association with it. On that profile the goal is telemetry and tracker reduction, full stop. uBlock Origin is the only extension I keep there, because every additional extension adds fingerprinting entropy and attack surface to the exact browser I use for banking.

The failure mode at every tier is mixing. The discipline is the protection, which is why most of the setup effort goes into making each tier pleasant enough that I never take the shortcut. A setup so painful that I work around it is less secure than a slightly looser one I follow exactly. Usability is a security property here rather than a concession to laziness.

What the VPN actually buys

Worth being precise, because a VPN is sold as something it is not. It is trust shifting rather than anonymity. I moved visibility from my ISP to Mullvad, a single party that can see my address and my destination at the same moment. That is a genuine gain against an ISP and against address based geolocation. It is not a substitute for Tor, and it does nothing about browser fingerprinting, which is the browser's job. The account is a random number with no email attached to it.

The GrapheneOS phone

Status: mostly built.

A Pixel 9a running GrapheneOS. The phone is the device most people carry into every room of their life, so it gets compartments rather than one trust boundary around the whole thing.

  • User profiles act as the compartments. The owner profile stays near empty. Identity apps live in one profile and pseudonymous activity lives in another, and an app in one profile cannot see the data in another.
  • Sandboxed Google Play is installed only in the profile that needs it, so Google account linkage does not span the whole device.
  • Location comes from the GNSS chip with Google network location switched off. The position fix happens on the device and nothing about it leaves. First fix is slower and that is the trade.
  • Maps are offline through CoMaps. Regions download once and after that navigation works in airplane mode, so there is no per search or per destination call telling anyone where I am going.
  • 2G is disabled, which mitigates the same downgrade attack the detection kit below exists to spot.

The wireless detection kit

Status: designed, hardware not acquired yet.

This is the counter surveillance half of the category 2 answer. The goal is situational awareness about what is transmitting near me, which covers Bluetooth trackers that follow me between locations and rogue access points and cell site simulators. No single affordable device sees the whole spectrum, so the bands decide how many devices the kit needs.

Band or threat Device What it gives
2.4 and 5 GHz WiFi. Rogue access points and hidden cameras. Alfa AWUS036ACM with Kismet Identification
Sub GHz through 6 GHz broadband. Unknown transmitters. HackRF One Detection of energy
Cellular. IMSI catchers and cell site simulators. Rayhunter on an Orbic hotspot Detection, passive
Bluetooth low energy trackers. AirGuard on the Pixel Detection, always on

Two modes matter and the kit needs both of them. Protocol aware tools like Kismet identify a device by its address and its encryption. The software defined radio does not decode consumer protocols at all, but it detects energy across a huge range, which is how you notice a transmitter you had no name for in the first place.

Everything stays in receive mode. Monitor mode and SDR receive and passive tracker scanning are all silent. Transmitting would make the kit itself detectable, and detection does not need it.

The ghost laptop

Status: planned.

Everything above is built for categories 1 and 2. Asking a comfortable daily driver to also beat a state level adversary does not work, because the attack surface that makes it a pleasant daily driver is exactly what a category 3 platform cannot afford. Pretending otherwise just degrades usability until I take a shortcut. So category 3 gets its own appliance and nothing crosses between the two.

The design is Tails on a write protected Libreboot T480. Tails supplies amnesia and forces all traffic through Tor. Libreboot supplies a trustworthy hardware base underneath it, closing the firmware persistence gap that Tails itself documents it cannot cover. me_cleaner and deguard between them remove the Intel management engine and disable Boot Guard, which leaves an auditable flash chip that can be locked.

The linchpin is that flash write protection, and it is not on by default. Libreboot ships write enabled so that updates stay easy, which means that until the flash is locked through the chipset protected range registers, the immutable firmware the entire design rests on does not actually exist. Enabling that protection can brick the board, so the external flasher and a known good ROM come before the first attempt rather than after it.

One rule governs the appliance. Category 3 activity happens there and nowhere else. No shared account. No shared vault. No shared username. No USB stick that has also touched the daily driver. A single crossing collapses the entire value of the split, which means the rule has to be absolute to be worth anything.

What it does not do

This belongs on the page before anyone relies on the design. Libreboot with Tails raises the cost against a state actor dramatically and it is worth building, but it is not immunity, and no consumer setup fully defeats a patient and well funded adversary with physical access. Flash write protection covers the boot firmware and not the embedded controller or the drive firmware. Descriptor protection can be defeated by strapping pins on an opened board, which is what a long border detention hands somebody. Tor acknowledges that a global passive adversary can correlate traffic by timing and volume. And the human layer deanonymizes people far more often than cryptography or firmware ever fails, through a reused handle or a leaked timezone or a writing style somebody recognizes.

Accounts and credentials

Status: in progress.

Device hardening does not survive a weak account layer, and this is the half I am working through now. Two ideas govern all of it.

The first is that email is the master key. Whoever controls the inbox can password reset their way into nearly everything downstream of it, so the mailbox deserves more protection than any single account it can reach. That means a hardware key on the mailbox and removing SMS as a recovery method, because SMS quietly reintroduces SIM swap as a backdoor around the hardware key I just added.

The second is that a second factor is only second if it is genuinely separate. TOTP seeds stored in the same vault as the passwords collapse two factors back into one, and the 2FA stops being 2FA the moment a single file compromise takes both. So the vault is KeePassXC opened by a passphrase and a key file that travels only by USB, and the seeds live in a different database entirely.

What the build taught me

The parts of this that look most technical are not the parts doing most of the work. Firmware and encryption raise the cost of attacking the hardware, but the browser tiers and the account hygiene and the discipline of not crossing streams cover far more of the risk I actually face. That maps back onto the enterprise work more directly than I expected it to. Least privilege and separation of duties are the same idea wearing different clothes, which is that you decide ahead of time what each context is allowed to reach, and then you do not make exceptions for your own convenience.
